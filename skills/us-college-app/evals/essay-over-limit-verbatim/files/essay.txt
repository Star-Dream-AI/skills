The first time I burned a batch of mooncakes, my grandmother laughed so hard she had to sit down on the flour sack. I was eleven, and I had been so sure that a hotter oven would make them bake faster. Instead the kitchen filled with smoke, the smoke alarm screamed, and twelve lotus seed mooncakes turned into twelve small black stones. My grandmother wiped her eyes, handed me a fresh bowl of dough, and said only one thing: start again, but slower this time.

I did not understand then that she was talking about more than baking. For years I treated every problem the way I treated that oven. When my grades in physics slipped in tenth grade, I turned up the heat. I stayed up until two in the morning working through practice problems I barely understood, copying solutions instead of asking why they worked. My scores did not improve. They got worse, and so did my sleep, my mood, and my patience with my younger brother, who only wanted me to help him build a paper boat.

The real setback came in the spring of eleventh grade. Our school robotics team, which I had joined because my best friend dragged me to the first meeting, was preparing for the provincial competition. I was in charge of the code that told our robot how to pick up small plastic cubes and drop them into a bin. Two days before the competition, the arm started shaking violently every time it moved. I did what I always did. I rewrote large parts of the program at once, late at night, hoping that speed and effort would fix it. By the morning of the competition the arm did not move at all.

We were eliminated in the first round. I remember standing in the hallway of the exhibition center, holding the robot's useless arm, feeling that I had let down five people who had trusted me. On the bus home nobody blamed me, which somehow made it worse. That night I could not sleep, so I went into the kitchen and, for the first time in years, made mooncakes with my grandmother.

She did not ask about the competition. She asked me to measure the syrup, and then to measure it again. She showed me how to press the dough evenly, how to wait for the oven to settle, how to check the color through the glass instead of opening the door every minute. Everything took longer than I wanted it to. But at the end of the night we had twelve perfect mooncakes, golden and glossy, and I realized that I had not felt so calm in months.

The next week I went back to the robot. Instead of rewriting everything, I changed one thing at a time and wrote down what happened after each change. It was slow and frustrating, and for three days nothing seemed to work. On the fourth day I found the problem: a single line that sent the motor two conflicting commands at the same moment. The fix took less than a minute. Finding it had taken patience I had never practiced before.

I have tried to bring that patience into the rest of my life. When I struggle with a physics problem now, I explain each step out loud to my brother, who pretends to be bored but has started asking his own questions. When our team prepared for this year's competition, I set up a simple testing log that every member uses, and we finished third in the province. When I volunteer at the community library on Saturdays, I let the children read at their own pace, even when the line of parents waiting behind them grows long.

I still burn things sometimes. Last month I left a pot of rice on the stove too long while I was reading about control systems, and my grandmother laughed at me again. But I no longer believe that turning up the heat is the same as working hard. The setback taught me that the fastest way to fix something is often to slow down, to look closely, and to change one thing at a time. My grandmother knew that all along. It just took a broken robot, a smoky kitchen, and a long bus ride home for me to learn it too.
